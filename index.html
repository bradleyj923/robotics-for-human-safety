Robotics for Human Safety

Good questions. Real examples. People worth protecting.

What could a robot do before we ask a person to step into danger?

A firefighter searching a smoke-filled building. A ranger climbing toward an injured person. A bystander waiting for lifesaving equipment. These are not abstract technology problems. They are moments when time, information and access matter.

This collection, curated by Bradley Shogren, brings together rescue stories, engineering examples and conversations with people building the future. It is for curious students, educators, responders, engineers and anyone who wants to turn “What if?” into a better question.

Why I care

I wanted to be a firefighter as a child, served as a volunteer firefighter in the 1990s, and remain connected to preparedness through Frisco CERT. The impulse to help does not disappear when you stop wearing the uniform.

I served for a couple of years with a suburban Minneapolis fire department, responding not only to fires but also to medical, CPR and trauma calls. That experience made the connection between a technical problem and a person needing help very concrete. Through CERT, I still stay connected to preparedness; recently I responded to a CPR alert and arrived to find others already providing care. Sometimes being ready to help matters even when you are not the person who ultimately performs the intervention.

My interest in science also grew through people. In the early 1990s, I lived in Akademgorodok near Novosibirsk, surrounded by a host family, friends and a community of scientists. I spent additional years in Novosibirsk. Those relationships gave me an appreciation for scientific work and for the people-to-people connections that make cooperation possible.

Entrepreneurship supplied another perspective. Running a supermarket in central Russia and working in restaurants made food waste tangible: something with a cost, a destination and potentially a better use. Today, helping European scientists explore black soldier fly larvae and circular-economy opportunities brings those interests together. Protein, fats and fertilizer are part of that story; chitin, melanin and peptides raise further questions about what value might be recovered. I am not a professional scientist, but I am a persistent question-asker who enjoys connecting people, problems and possibilities.

My high-school friend Phil Otis died during a mountain rescue. Years later, in business school, I wondered whether a wearable device could tell rescuers precisely where a firefighter had fallen inside a building. I encountered a technical barrier and did not pursue the idea. Looking back, the question I wish I had asked was: “Is this impossible, or does it need a different approach?”

Those experiences inform this collection. They are not a claim to have engineered the solution. They are a reason to keep asking what technology could make possible.

A useful progression

Alert → Locate → Navigate → Assess → Assist

A loud alarm matters. A precise location adds something different. A safe route, reliable communication and useful assistance add still more. Each step offers a research question. Sometimes the right answer is an autonomous robot. Sometimes it is a remotely operated machine, a better sensor or a simpler interface.

The spirit of this project

- Protect people, including the people who come to help.

- Start with the need, not the most impressive demonstration.

- Learn across disciplines and borders. Human safety benefits from scientific exchange and trustworthy shared standards.

- Ask what works in the field. Weather, smoke, stairs, weak signals, cost and training are part of the design.

- Keep curiosity and evidence together. These pages distinguish deployed tools, studies and ideas worth testing without treating today's limits as permanent.

WHY IT MATTERS

When an alarm is not a location

A question that stayed with me

Bradley Shogren's recollection, edited for this collection.

In the 1990s, I wore a PASS device as a volunteer firefighter. If it was activated and the wearer stopped moving, it would sound a piercing alarm. The hope was that another firefighter could hear it and come to help.

Now imagine a large building. A responder is down on an upper floor, far from the rest of the crew. An alarm may be sounding, but where should help go? Which floor? Which side? Which corridor? A floor number alone is not much help when the floor spans a city block.

Around 2001–02, while exploring entrepreneurship ideas in business school, I wondered whether a wearable device could give colleagues that location. I discussed the technical difficulty with a friend and did not pursue it. I am not telling this story to blame the advice or claim that I could have solved the engineering. I am telling it because a useful question survived even when I stopped working on it.

“We cannot do this reliably with this approach” is not the same as “We can never do this.”

What the engineering tells us

GPS already supported vertical positioning in 2001. The challenge was not a universal ban on measuring height. It was obtaining a reliable, precise location inside buildings, including the right floor. Satellite signals, steel and concrete, changing conditions and the practical needs of responders make that a different problem from locating a car outdoors. The 2001 GPS performance standard included vertical performance; the FAA: how GPS works.

A NIST-funded indoor-location project combined ultra-wideband ranging, movement sensing, radios and pressure measurements. NIST's NIST: tracking responders describes continued progress and the difficulty of dependable tracking in complex buildings.

The interesting question becomes: What measurements can compensate for one another when any single sensor is imperfect?

Add a robot to the question

Could a robot accompany a crew, map the route and help maintain a communications link? Could it carry or place localization beacons? Could it go first into an uncertain space and return information that changes the commander's decision?

These are design questions, not a single promised product. Try separating them:

1. Alert: detect that something may be wrong.

2. Locate: report a position and how uncertain it is.

3. Navigate: identify a usable route to that position.

4. Assess: determine what conditions the rescue team will face.

5. Assist: carry equipment, relay communication or perform a task.

Remembering Phil

Phil Otis was my friend from high school. The National Park Service memorial records that Phil, a 22-year-old Student Conservation Association volunteer ranger, died while responding to an injured climber on Mount Rainier on August 12, 1995. Ranger Sean Ryan also died during that rescue.

Remembering them invites a forward-looking question: Can we get information or equipment to a person in danger while reducing the risk to those coming to help? A small aircraft, ground robot or combination might someday help with part of a mountain mission. The terrain, weather, payload, communications and actual rescue still have to be solved. The point is not to claim a machine would have changed that tragedy. It is to honor the purpose of rescue by continuing to improve the tools.

Your turn

Think of a problem someone you know has experienced. What part is a physical limitation? What part is a limitation of today's equipment? What would you measure first? Who has firsthand knowledge that could change your design?

You do not have to begin with the answer. Begin with a reason to care, then keep asking better questions.

RESCUE & RESPONSE

Six examples that turn curiosity into engineering

Start with the human need. Then ask what the machine, the operator and the rest of the response system must do together.

1. Bring an AED before the ambulance

If a drone can deliver dinner, could it deliver a chance to survive? Imagine a cardiac arrest three blocks from a school. An automated external defibrillator (AED) is nearby, but inside a building. What if a 911 call could launch a dedicated AED drone while the dispatcher guides CPR and the ambulance is already on its way?

Why this is personal for Brad

As a volunteer firefighter in suburban Minneapolis in the 1990s, Brad often helped with CPR while waiting for paramedics and their defibrillator. He remembers performing chest compressions or helping provide oxygen during many unsuccessful resuscitation attempts. He does not recall a survivor among the people he personally helped resuscitate. Those memories make the minutes before equipment arrives feel very different from numbers on a chart. They are why the possibility of faster AED delivery matters to him.

CPR and defibrillation work together. CPR helps keep blood flowing to the brain and heart. An AED analyzes the rhythm and advises or delivers a shock when appropriate; not every cardiac arrest has a shockable rhythm. Faster access matters, but it complements CPR and emergency medical care rather than replacing them. Follow 911 dispatcher and AED instructions, and do not interrupt CPR to go searching for a device when someone else can retrieve it.

What a minute can mean: The American Heart Association's 2025 guidelines cite a registry study of witnessed out-of-hospital arrests with an initial rhythm of ventricular fibrillation. Each additional minute to the first shock was associated with a 6% lower probability of survival to hospital discharge. This is an observed relationship in that group, not six percentage points subtracted from every patient's chances each minute. AHA 2025 guidelines, section 9.2

This part is already being tested

Sweden: In a study published in 2023, drones delivered AEDs before ambulances in 37 of the 55 cases with comparable arrival times, gaining a median 3 minutes and 14 seconds in those earlier deliveries. Bystanders attached the delivered AED before EMS arrival in six confirmed cardiac arrests. The important next step is not merely getting equipment to the address, but getting it into use.

United States: Duke Health reported in November 2025 that a clinical trial in Clemmons, North Carolina, was dispatching AED drones during real 911 calls, alongside EMS. A dispatcher coordinates with a drone pilot, the aircraft lowers the AED by winch, and the dispatcher guides a bystander in retrieving and using it. That is remarkably close to Brad's starting question, not a scenario that must wait until 2050.

The next design challenge: a neighborhood response network

Brad's idea is to think beyond one aircraft: could schools, fire stations, police stations, pharmacies or rural community hubs host a network that brings AEDs to nearby homes and public places? Treat these as possible sites to evaluate, not instructions to remove the only AED a school needs.

  • Place it where minutes matter. Map travel times, cardiac-arrest locations and communities poorly served by existing response.
  • Make it ready when the building is closed. Consider dedicated outdoor equipment, batteries, maintenance and safe delivery in wind, rain and darkness.
  • Keep the whole response working. Coordinate dispatch and flight permissions, retain on-site AED coverage, and design a handoff that does not pull the only rescuer away from CPR.
  • Measure useful help, not impressive flight. How much sooner are the pads attached? Can a frightened bystander find and use the equipment?

From equipment on a wall to a resource on a network

Brad's analogy is the parked car: what if an asset waiting in one place could serve someone who needs it elsewhere? Applied to emergency equipment, the goal is not to keep every AED busy. A device waiting for an emergency is doing its job. The opportunity is to connect that readiness to a larger neighborhood without leaving its home site unprotected.

A building block already exists. PulsePoint's National Emergency AED + Resource (NEAR) Registry connects resource locations with emergency communication centers and records access information. In participating communities, AED-needed alerts ask subscribed people to bring a registered device to a nearby emergency. These alerts use the AED's location, not the subscriber's personal location. That is a useful companion to the location-aware responder concept that inspired Brad, but it is not an automatic drone-launch service.

The question worth building around. Could a 911 system match a confirmed incident location with the fastest appropriate, ready resource, then coordinate a person or an authorized drone delivery? The nearest pin on a map may not be the fastest usable AED. The system would need to know whether the cabinet is accessible, the device and aircraft are ready, the route is safe, and someone can receive the package. A dispatcher-supervised network could automate the matching while keeping emergency judgment and fallback arrangements clear.

  • Technical readiness. Design trustworthy status updates, secure dispatch commands, accurate delivery locations and a safe response to lost communications. Test the full chain from call to usable equipment, including failures, not just successful flights.
  • Access and operating responsibility. Who can release the equipment, who receives it, and who replaces used pads or restores the kit afterward? Plan for locked buildings, nighttime access, languages, disability access and overlapping emergencies. A dedicated community delivery AED may be better than borrowing a school's only unit.
  • Policy and sustained funding. Agree on ownership, medical oversight, aviation approvals, privacy, liability, staffing and maintenance before launch. Include communities with few resources, not only neighborhoods that can afford the hardware.

The same design question could extend to bleeding-control kits or other first-aid supplies. NEAR already lists bleeding-control kits, epinephrine and naloxone alongside AEDs; that does not establish that any particular item is approved for drone delivery or appropriate for every caller. Each proposed payload needs its own clinical purpose, storage requirements, trained support and delivery plan. The student challenge: choose one community, one emergency need and one measurable improvement, then build a small simulation before proposing a fleet.

That adds harder tasks: reaching the patient, gaining access to the chest, placing pads and supporting safe clinical decisions. For a young engineer, the invitation is to choose one missing step and ask how it could become safer, simpler or faster.

2. Put a machine in the risky position

Evidence: deployed firefighting equipment. The Los Angeles Fire Department introduced the remotely operated Thermite RS3 to deliver water while keeping an operator farther from a hazardous position. It demonstrates that useful robotics does not require complete autonomy. LAFD introduction and visuals

Ask: Which task needs a person physically present, and which needs human judgment from a safer location?

The RS3 changes where a person needs to stand to apply judgment and useful force. It suggests a practical kind of progress: let the machine bear more exposure while trained responders stay involved in the decision. A remotely operated system can be a major advance without pretending to replace the crew.

3. Make an unfamiliar building understandable

Evidence: development and testing. Indoor-drone mapping work highlighted by NIST explores how images and maps can help incident commanders. The hard parts include narrow spaces, limited flight time and environments where GPS is unavailable. NIST's mapping story

Ask: What does a responder need on a map that a beautiful 3D model might not show?

A map becomes valuable when it reduces uncertainty at the moment a decision must be made. Think beyond impressive imagery to routes, obstacles, confidence and information a responder can actually absorb under pressure.

4. Sense the hazard without sending someone toward it

Evidence: technology development. NASA-supported Squishy Robotics developed drop-resistant sensor robots for hazardous environments. A device left near a hazard can provide a different view than a drone passing overhead. NASA story and images

Ask: Which measurements would change the decision to approach, evacuate or wait? How would uncertainty be displayed?

Squishy Robotics invites a different mental picture of a robot: not necessarily something that walks, but something that survives arrival and reports from a place people should not yet enter. A small package of trustworthy information can change a large decision about exposure and response.

5. Turn readings into an earlier warning

Evidence: AI research. NIST researchers have investigated machine-learning models that predict flashover using temperature information. This is an example of interpreting measurements, not simply adding another camera. NIST research overview

Ask: How early must an alert arrive to be useful? What are the costs of missed warnings and frequent false alarms?

Flashover is a dangerous transition in a compartment fire, when conditions can escalate rapidly. The promise of prediction is time to recognize danger and act, rather than a more detailed account after the event. That makes the quality and timing of a warning as important as the intelligence of the model.

6. Make an ordinary factory task less difficult

Evidence: company announcement and investment. Nidus announced $30 million in funding in November 2026 to develop AI-enabled robotics for existing manufacturing workflows, beginning with wire harnesses. Flexible wires are a revealing challenge: they bend, move and do not behave like rigid blocks. Nidus announcement

Ask: Why might a repetitive task still be difficult to automate? What would the robot have to sense instead of assume? Nidus's defense focus also provides a concrete opening for the dual-use discussion.

A wire harness makes an excellent lesson in the gap between repetition and simplicity. Humans adapt to flexible objects almost without noticing; a robot must sense and manage those variations. Solving an unglamorous task can unlock meaningful industrial value.

Test every idea against the real world

Dimension · A better question

Time · How long from unpacking to useful information or action?

Reliability · What happens when a sensor disagrees or a radio link drops?

Access · Can it handle stairs, rubble, mud, water or confined spaces?

Human use · Can someone operate it with gloves, noise and divided attention?

Economics · Can the intended organization afford, maintain and train with it?

Value · What measurable risk, delay or burden does it reduce?

AI may help with perception, planning and interpretation. Remote control may be better for some tasks. Choose the technology after defining success.

HEALTH & DAILY LIFE

A robot does not have to enter a fire to change a life

Mobility, independence and connection

The quieter opportunities may be just as consequential: reaching a dropped object, walking with support, participating in a family activity, or having an easier way to contact another person. The design question is not simply “What can the robot do?” It is “What does this let a person choose to do for themselves?”

Wearable robotics: three different ambitions

Assist movement. Practice movement. Recover movement. These are not interchangeable promises. An exoskeleton may provide mechanical assistance while worn; rehabilitation may use repeated, supported practice; recovery is a clinical outcome that must be measured. Keeping those goals separate makes the engineering more interesting, not less.

ReWalk 7: a real personal exoskeleton

FDA-cleared device · specific users and settings

The FDA's March 2025 record describes a powered frame with hip and knee motors, controls and crutches. Its home-and-community indication covers people with spinal cord injury at T7–L5, with assessment, training and supervision by a specially certified companion. A separate T4–T6 indication applies in rehabilitation institutions. Its specified activities include standing, walking on level surfaces and mild slopes, and negotiating stairs and curbs. FDA record

That is substantial progress beyond a laboratory video. It also raises practical questions: How does someone put it on? How much help is required? What happens at a narrow doorway, on a wet surface or when power runs low? Those are central design requirements, not afterthoughts.

Honda Walking Assist: training rather than a universal replacement

Established rehabilitation example

Honda's device uses powered hip assistance for walking rehabilitation. The FDA summary specifies stroke patients who meet walking criteria and use it under trained professional supervision in rehabilitation facilities. It is a useful contrast with a personal exoskeleton: helping someone practice a gait is a different job from supporting all of everyday mobility. FDA summary · Honda explanation

Student challenge: Sketch a control system that helps only as much as needed. What would it measure to distinguish a person's intention from fatigue, imbalance or an obstacle?

Independence can start with one small action

Hello Robot's Stretch Assist program explores home support with people who have mobility impairments. Its current page describes remote testing and a small in-home co-design program involving engineers and an occupational therapist; it says Stretch 4 is not yet available for individual purchase. The company's participant stories emphasize independence and social participation, not merely mechanical dexterity. Stretch Assist

Imagine choosing an object, bringing it closer and deciding where to put it without waiting for someone else. Success would require much more than a gripper: a usable interface, reliable object recognition, safe force limits, a way to stop, and an understanding of the user's preferences. A “small” task can be enormous to the person whose choice it restores.

Try a low-risk project: Interview someone about an inconvenient daily task. Build a cardboard or software prototype of the interface first. Ask what control they want to keep, what assistance they welcome, and what would feel intrusive. Never assume that a researcher’s preferred solution is the user’s preferred life.

Can a machine help someone feel less alone?

There are two different ideas here: a robot as a conversational companion, and a robot as a bridge to other people. They can overlap, but should not be confused.

A 2025 randomized study in Japan recruited 73 older adults living alone; 68 completed follow-up. Over four weeks, the BOCCO emo robot facilitated communication with human operators and family members and provided reminders. The intervention group had a greater reduction in measured loneliness than the control group. This was a short study of a particular system, not proof that any chatbot will have the same effect. The human connection built into the intervention is especially worth noticing. Read the study

New York's Office for the Aging also describes its real-world ElliQ companion program. Its program reports are useful implementation evidence, distinct from a randomized comparison. New York program

The promising question is not “Can we replace friendship?” It is “Can technology make it easier to remain engaged, initiate contact and participate in life?” Measure human contact and user choice alongside engagement with the device. More minutes spent talking to a machine is not, by itself, the same as a better life.

A familiar voice: comfort, consent and honesty

Consider a general design idea: a person chooses a familiar, authorized voice for an assistive system. It might make an interface easier to recognize or more welcoming. But familiarity can also increase trust in something that has not earned it.

A responsible design would obtain the voice owner's explicit permission, make the synthetic nature clear, allow consent to be withdrawn, and avoid implying that the real person is speaking live. It would also ask the listener what they prefer. A saved recording, an actual call and newly generated speech are three different things.

Discuss: Could the system offer a real call when someone wants company? Who controls recordings and generated speech? How would it avoid fabricated memories, medical instructions or promises in another person's voice? Is a warm original voice a better choice than a clone?

The goal is assistance that preserves dignity and agency. A successful robot should make room for more life, not quietly narrow the world around its user.

SPACE & INDUSTRY

Different environments. The same engineering questions.

Space, construction and connected care

A construction robot and a robot approaching an icy crevasse seem worlds apart. Both need to understand where they are, cope with an imperfect environment and do something useful without causing harm. Looking across fields can reveal a solution already taking shape somewhere else.

Space: where people cannot easily go

A snake-inspired explorer

Research and development

NASA JPL's EELS, the Exobiology Extant Life Surveyor, takes inspiration from a snake. Its prototypes combine sensing with screw-like propulsion to explore difficult terrain. The vision includes investigating openings in icy worlds, while the engineering starts with tests here on Earth. It is a development project, not a launched mission. JPL EELS project · Watch EELS testing

Ask: What body shape lets a machine enter a place people cannot? When would a flexible, segmented body be more useful than wheels or legs? Could the same idea help inspect a confined space on Earth?

An asteroid sample, brought home

Completed sample-return achievement

NASA's OSIRIS-REx returned material from asteroid Bennu to Earth on September 24, 2023. That is a concrete example of a robotic spacecraft doing difficult physical work far from its operators: reaching a target, collecting a sample and delivering it for study. NASA OSIRIS-REx mission

What has to happen onboard when instructions cannot arrive instantly? What should be simulated before departure? What information must a machine send back so people can decide the next step?

Construction: precision without a humanoid

Mortenson and autonomous heavy equipment

Use on selected renewable-energy projects

Mortenson describes its partnership with Built Robotics to use autonomous heavy equipment on selected renewable-energy projects. The machine does not need to imitate a person. It needs to perform a defined construction task reliably within a larger worksite operation. Mortenson partnership

Ask: Which repetitive tasks are suitable for automation? How are work areas controlled, exceptions handled and responsibilities divided between people and machines? A productive machine is only useful if the whole site can work safely around it.

Turning a model into marks on a floor

Supplier-documented construction use

Dusty Robotics describes Mortenson using robotic layout on two data-center projects. The task translates digital building information into physical layout that different trades can use. This is a bridge between software and the built world, rather than a robot that builds a building by itself. Dusty: Mortenson case study

Try: Draw a room plan, then ask how a robot would establish its position and transfer that plan onto the real floor. What happens when the floor is obstructed or the digital plan changes? The most valuable innovation may be reducing rework and coordination mistakes.

Surgical robotics: control, distance and trust

A surgeon at the controls

Established clinical technology

Intuitive describes its da Vinci systems as surgeon-controlled: the surgeon operates the camera and instruments through the system. That is different from a machine independently deciding how to perform an operation. Intuitive: how da Vinci works

Ask: How can a machine extend a clinician's precision while preserving clear human control? Which information does the surgeon need, and how should the system communicate a problem?

A long-distance research demonstration

Animal research

In 2024, ETH Zurich and the Chinese University of Hong Kong demonstrated magnetic endoscopy controlled from Zurich in an anesthetized pig in Hong Kong, about 9,300 kilometers away. ETH reports a delay of roughly 300 milliseconds. This was a research demonstration, not routine remote human care. ETH: remote endoscopy

The collaboration is as interesting as the distance. It brings together expertise in different places to test a difficult problem. And a faster network is not the whole answer: control, visualization, local clinical capability and a response to interruption all matter.

Discuss: What should remain under local control? How would the system behave if the connection degraded? How should a remote expert and an on-site team share responsibility?

What next-generation connectivity could add

The ITU's 2026 discussion of IMT-2030, the framework for 6G, includes reliability, low latency, AI and integrated sensing in developing technical requirements. Those goals make interesting research questions for robotics, but the ETH demonstration already worked without waiting for 6G. ITU: the 6G framework

Ask what performance a particular task actually requires, then test the entire system. A network label does not by itself establish that a clinical or safety-critical application is ready.

Look for the transferable idea

A construction layout robot teaches us about translating a model into reality. A space robot teaches us about delayed communication. A medical system teaches us about precise action near vulnerable tissue. A student does not have to master all three fields to notice that the questions overlap.

LEARN FROM NATURE

The next idea may already be in front of us

Look closely. Ask differently. Test what you notice.

A bird adjusts its wings in a gust. A gecko crosses a surface that looks impossible to grip. Insects turn discarded organic material into new biological resources. These are ordinary scenes until somebody looks at them as engineering questions.

That is the invitation of biomimicry: study how living systems accomplish something, identify the mechanism, and ask whether that principle can help solve a human problem. It is not simply making a robot look like an animal. Sometimes the useful lesson is in a surface, a feedback loop, an energy-saving movement or the way a system handles damage.

From a gecko's foot to a robot's grasp

NASA describes a direct path from research on gecko-inspired adhesion to commercial robotic grippers. Instead of relying on conventional clamping or suction, the design draws on the interaction between tiny structures and a surface. NASA's account describes industrial handling of smooth objects such as circuit boards and solar panels. NASA: gecko-inspired grippers

The interesting leap is not “a gecko is amazing.” It is: What lets it attach, release and repeat, and what must change when we build that mechanism ourselves? That question connects biology, materials science, manufacturing and robotics.

An animal is a teacher, not a blueprint

MIT's account of Sangbae Kim's work describes a cheetah-inspired robot whose electric motors produced different mechanics from the animal. Inspiration led to investigation, not a literal copy. MIT: bioinspired robotics

Ask what matters: the shape of the leg, the timing of contact, the distribution of mass, or the ability to recover after a slip? A good analogy helps you choose an experiment. It does not have to be a perfect analogy to be useful.

From an ant question to a caterpillar design

A student's interest in arterial imaging prompted Brad to wonder whether ants and their tunnels might offer clues about narrow structures that stay open. The research trail led somewhere different: a published caterpillar-inspired stent-graft design. Its authors studied a segmented structure inspired by caterpillar cuticle and evaluated its mechanical behavior in laboratory testing. Caterpillar stent-graft study

This does not make ant tunnels and arteries the same engineering problem. It illustrates something more useful: an initial analogy can open a search that finds a better one. The next question becomes specific: how can a tubular structure combine flexibility, support and resistance to kinking? AI can help uncover the neighboring research, while the scientist decides what is relevant and tests it.

A library nature has been building all along

Michael Wright's essay, “AI Can Now Search Four Billion Years of Engineering. What Is That Worth?”, asks how biological insight becomes something engineers and organizations can actually use. His central challenge is to move from admiring nature's designs to finding, comparing and applying the mechanisms behind them. Michael Wright's essay

For a student, that is an invitation to search by function: attach, filter, protect, cool, sense, move. A useful answer may come from an organism you would never have thought to study. Brad knows Michael through their shared board work in citizen diplomacy, another reminder that ideas often travel through relationships before they travel into a laboratory.

Two ledgers: what we record and what physically happens

A ledger is a record of what comes in, what goes out and what is owed. Brad uses the two-ledger idea to distinguish our human records from nature's physical accounting.

The human ledger contains budgets, contracts, deadlines, policy choices and the benefits we decide to measure. The physical ledger contains the energy used, heat released, material moved, loads carried, waste produced and biological effects that actually occur.

We can revise a budget, change a deadline or decide not to count a cost. That does not make the physical effect disappear. A rescue robot still needs enough energy to return. A structure still has to carry its load. Waste still goes somewhere. The lesson is not that economics or policy are unimportant. It is that durable solutions have to work in both ledgers.

Try it: For a new robot, draw two columns. In one, put purchase price, maintenance, staffing and time saved. In the other, put battery use, heat, materials, repair needs and end-of-life waste. What benefit looks different when you look at both columns together?

Curiosity comes before credentials

Brad is not a scientist. His contribution here is the habit of noticing a connection, asking whether it might matter, and inviting someone with deeper expertise to investigate. His lobster-shell and battery question is an example of that kind of spark: can a biological structure suggest a way to manage a materials problem? The useful next step is to identify the actual mechanism, search the research and refine the question.

AI can help with that middle step. It can find unfamiliar terminology, suggest neighboring fields, compare explanations and surface experiments. Then the researcher checks the sources and tests the idea. Being close enough to ask a promising question can be a valuable beginning.

The music is all around us

“The music is all around us. All you have to do is listen.”

: August Rush, the character played by Freddie Highmore in the 2007 film August Rush. Watch the closing moment

For Brad, that is a way to think about scientific curiosity. The world is full of mechanisms we have not yet understood, useful relationships we have not noticed, and questions we have not learned how to ask. A creature gripping a wall, a wing adjusting in the wind, or a material flexing without breaking can be the beginning of an investigation.

The laws of nature keep operating whether we pay attention or not. A difficult problem does not disappear when we stop working on it, and an unanswered question does not become permanently unanswerable just because today's approach failed. Listening, in this analogy, means observing carefully, exploring unfamiliar fields, studying what others have learned and putting an idea to the test.

There is room for many kinds of listeners. A biologist might recognize the mechanism, an engineer might find a way to build it, a clinician might see a meaningful use, and a student might ask the question none of them had considered. Scientists across borders can contribute complementary insights. The invitation is not to assume that nature offers an easy answer to every problem. It is to stay curious enough to notice when a promising clue is already in front of us.

What do animals do effortlessly that machines struggle to do? And what might you notice tomorrow that changes the question you ask today?

Stay with the question long enough to learn

“I have no special talents. I am only passionately curious.” Einstein wrote those words to Carl Seelig on March 11, 1952, as documented in Princeton University Press’s quotation collection. The important invitation is not to compare yourself with Einstein. It is to take your own questions seriously enough to investigate them. Einstein: source collection

“I know several thousand things that won’t work!” is the Edison remark recalled by his associate Walter S. Mallory in the Rutgers Edison Papers discussion of storage-battery experiments. Rutgers: Edison and persistence

An unsuccessful experiment can still be useful if it tells you why an approach failed and helps you choose a better test. Persistence is not repeating the same attempt blindly. It is staying engaged while your understanding changes. You do not need a famous quotation to give yourself permission to begin.

BUILD A BETTER FUTURE

What kind of future are we building?

A discussion, not a test with one correct answer

Brad's organizing idea is mutually assured prosperity: use scientific and technological capability to make people safer and better off, rather than treating every advance as a way to threaten someone else.

That does not remove difficult security questions. Robotics can inspect a collapsed building or serve a military mission. A mapping system can guide a rescuer or reveal sensitive infrastructure. An international supply chain can expand access and introduce dependencies. Good design means looking directly at both benefit and risk.

Citizen diplomacy, one conversation at a time

For Brad, citizen diplomacy means building human relationships across borders: listening, exchanging ideas and finding shared work without requiring agreement on every political question. Mutually assured prosperity is the constructive ambition behind that approach. Scientists, students, responders and engineers can ask how to make cooperation useful and trustworthy while taking real security concerns seriously.

From mutually assured destruction to mutually assured prosperity

Mutually assured destruction is a logic of deterrence: the expectation of devastating retaliation is meant to prevent an attack. Brad's phrase, mutually assured prosperity, asks a different design question: what shared benefits make cooperation worth sustaining? Better disaster response, safer infrastructure and more accessible care can give people in different countries a practical stake in one another's success.

That is an aspiration, not a claim that conflicts or security risks disappear. A rescue drone may collect detailed building maps. A home assistant may record intimate conversations. A hospital robot may depend on remote software updates. Cooperation works better when those concerns become explicit requirements: control where data goes, authenticate operators, restrict privileges, verify updates, test loss of connectivity, and preserve a safe local fallback.

The constructive choice is not blind trust versus total separation. It is deciding what to share, what to protect, and how to demonstrate that a system deserves trust. Joint testing and clear standards can help useful ideas travel without asking people to ignore real risks.

Five conversations worth having

1. Shared problems, shared knowledge

A fire, earthquake or cardiac arrest does not respect national boundaries. Which research findings, safety tests and interoperability standards could researchers share across borders? Where are there legitimate reasons to restrict access?

2. Trust that can be tested

Would a common test course for smoke, rubble, stairs and weak communications make it easier to compare machines from different countries? NIST's response-robot work illustrates how missions can be translated into measurable capabilities.

3. Useful cooperation without blind trust

How could a public-safety organization evaluate cybersecurity, software updates, data access, repairability and supplier reliability consistently? What should be demonstrated rather than assumed, regardless of a vendor's nationality?

4. Dual use and responsibility

If a technology has both protective and destructive uses, what can its designers control? What should purchasers disclose? How could a team decide which applications it will support?

5. Who gets the benefits?

A breakthrough that only the richest institutions can afford leaves many people behind. Could simpler designs, shared training, modular parts or open test methods widen access without sacrificing safety?

Yesterday's limitation is not a permanent boundary

Think about Brad's example of AI-generated pictures: awkward hands and extra fingers became an easy shorthand for what image models could not do well. As the tools improved, that shorthand became less useful. The lesson is not that every difficult problem will disappear. It is that we should keep testing our assumptions instead of turning a limitation we once observed into a permanent rule.

The same habit matters in robotics. An unreliable location estimate today can become a question about a different sensor, a better map, or a combination of measurements. AI can help search unfamiliar fields, compare approaches and sharpen the question. The next step is still an experiment that tells us something real.

Brad would rather see scientists across borders working on common challenges than assume progress must belong to one country at another's expense. Cooperation and careful testing can reinforce each other: share what helps people, establish trustworthy standards, and examine risks openly.

For a curious student: What changed since somebody last said your idea would not work? What still has not changed? What small test could separate the two?

A 15-minute design challenge

Choose a need: find a downed responder; deliver a sensor to an unsafe location; help locate a stranded climber; bring equipment to a bystander.

- Minutes 1–3: Define the person being helped and the decision that needs to improve.

- Minutes 4–7: Sketch the smallest useful system. Include people, communications and equipment, not just the robot.

- Minutes 8–10: Break it on paper. Remove GPS, interrupt the radio, add bad weather, or make a sensor uncertain.

- Minutes 11–13: Name one measurable test and one person with experience you would interview.

- Minutes 14–15: Explain what you would try next and what would make you change your mind.

Leave with a next question, not merely a prediction. Curiosity becomes powerful when it leads to observation, testing and learning.
